# One More Rep

> "The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way."
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 5.20 (trans. Gregory Hays)

## The Rep After the Last Rep

Every set has a moment where the bar slows down. Your mind says the set is over. Your body usually has one more. Training lives in the gap between those two answers.

The rep after you wanted to stop is the one that teaches you the most. The others confirm what you already knew you could do. Chase the confirming reps and you stay the same. Chase the one more and you change.

## How Long the Forge Takes

Habits do not lock in after three weeks. Phillippa Lally and colleagues at University College London followed volunteers through a twelve-week habit study; for the participants whose progress could be modelled, the estimated time to near-full automaticity had a median of 66 days and ran from 18 to 254 days (*European Journal of Social Psychology*, 2010). Missing a single day did not materially change the process. Plan for a ten-week block, not a three-week one.

## The One More Protocol

Run this for ten weeks. Pick one movement you already train, such as push-ups, pull-ups or goblet squats. Find the number you can do with good form today and write it down.

Each session, do your number. Rest two minutes. Then do one more set and stop at one rep beyond the point where you wanted to quit. Log both numbers.

When your first set beats the old number on three sessions in a row, raise the number by one. If form breaks, the rep does not count. Stop the set there.

## When the Bar Will Not Move

Some weeks the number will not rise. Sleep, food and stress all show up on the bar. Hold the number and keep showing up.

A stalled week is still a training week. Deload for seven days at half volume. Then start again at your last logged number.

## The Standard

One more rep, every session, logged. If you can do one more with clean form, you do it.
