All example products below are fictional. Each example assumes its claim is in the claim ledger.
If your ledger does not support the claim, the pattern still applies but the example does not.

## 1. The rubric

Three graded criteria (0, 1 or 2 points each, six points maximum) and five gates (pass or fail).
A headline that fails any gate is cut, whatever it scores.

### Graded

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Specific** | A feeling or a category ("better work") | A domain ("rotas for cafés") | A situation, object or number the reader recognises ("next week's rota in under a minute") |
| **Verifiable** | Contains a claim with no ledger row | Claims are soft but true ("simple") | Every claim maps to a sourced ledger row |
| **Names the buyer or the outcome** | Could be for anyone | Buyer or outcome is implied | A reader knows in one second whether it is for them |

### Gates

| Gate | Fails when |
|---|---|
| **No stock AI words** | Contains unlock, elevate, seamless, supercharge, empower, transform, revolutionise, reimagine, next-level, effortless, game-changer, or a word on the humanizer's banned list |
| **No not-X-but-Y** | "It's not X, it's Y", "Not just X", "More than X", "X without the Y" used as a contrast reveal |
| **No colon-reveal** | "Thing, reimagined: meet Product", "The secret to X: Y", or the same shape with a dash |
| **Sentence case** | Any word other than the first and proper nouns is capitalised |
| **Hero length** | A hero headline has nine words or more (count hyphenated words as one, numbers as one) |

The hero-length gate applies to hero headlines only. Email subjects and section headings follow
section 5.

### Scoring sheet

Use this shape for all ten. It is what goes into the output for the two you keep.

```text
#  Headline                                    Spec Ver Buyer  Gates  Total
1  Next week's rota, built from who's free      2    2   1     pass   5
2  Unlock effortless scheduling                 0    1   0     FAIL   cut (stock words)
```

## 2. How to run the ten

- Write all ten before scoring any. Scoring while writing narrows you to one shape.
- Use at least four different patterns from section 3. Ten variations of one line is one
  candidate.
- Write at least two from the most specific claim in the ledger and two from the buyer's own
  words (support tickets, interview notes, reviews of the thing they use now).
- Ties: prefer the one with fewer words, then the one a buyer could repeat to a colleague.
- If nothing scores above 3, the ledger is too thin. Say that, rather than polishing a weak line.

## 3. Twelve patterns that work

| # | Pattern | Example | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The outcome with a time** | "Your invoices matched by Monday morning" | Names the result and when it arrives |
| 2 | **Name the buyer** | "Bookkeeping for Dutch freelancers who hate spreadsheets" | The right reader self-selects in one glance |
| 3 | **The verb they would use** | "Send the quote before you leave the site" | Written as the buyer describes the job, not as the vendor does |
| 4 | **The sourced number** | "Month-end close cut from 40 minutes to 6" | Concrete, checkable, and the ledger carries the method |
| 5 | **Input to output** | "Receipts in, filed tax return out" | Shows the whole job in six words |
| 6 | **The buyer's own question** | "Which subscriptions am I still paying for?" | Lifted from what buyers actually type; the page answers it |
| 7 | **Plain category plus one difference** | "A password manager that works offline" | Locates the product, then gives one reason to choose it |
| 8 | **The constraint as the promise** | "Runs on the laptop you already own" | Removes the objection before it forms |
| 9 | **The objection, answered** | "Cancel from settings, no email needed" | Speaks to the fear that stops the click |
| 10 | **The thing itself, named** | "Weekly meal plans for four, with the shopping list" | No metaphor; the reader sees exactly what arrives |
| 11 | **Proof as the headline** | "Used in 212 primary schools across Flanders" | When the proof is the strongest claim, lead with it (source required) |
| 12 | **The chore, ended** | "Stop chasing late rent by text" | Names a specific annoyance the buyer has today |

Notes on use:

- Patterns 4 and 11 depend entirely on the ledger. With no source, do not use them.
- Pattern 6 only works when the question is real. Invented questions read as pattern 9 in the
  next table.
- Pattern 12 is imperative. Use it when the chore is vivid. "Stop wasting time" is not vivid.

## 4. Twelve patterns that read as AI, with the fix

| # | Pattern | Example of the tell | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Not-X-but-Y** | "It's not a scheduler. It's a teammate." | Say the Y with a concrete noun: "Builds the rota from who's free" |
| 2 | **Colon-reveal** | "Scheduling, reimagined: meet Shiftbook" | Drop the setup, state the job: "Next week's rota in under a minute" |
| 3 | **Stock verb** | "Unlock your team's potential" | Name the action the product performs: "Swap shifts without a group chat" |
| 4 | **Adjective stack** | "Seamless, intelligent, effortless" | Replace with one concrete noun phrase: "One rota, every location" |
| 5 | **The future of X** | "The future of hospitality staffing" | Say what is true today: "Rotas for cafés with 3 to 15 staff" |
| 6 | **All-in-one platform** | "Your all-in-one workforce platform" | Lead with the one job it does best; list the rest lower down |
| 7 | **Title Case Everything** | "Build Better Rotas Faster" | Sentence case: "Build next week's rota faster" (and then make "faster" concrete) |
| 8 | **Three fragments** | "Faster. Smarter. Simpler." | One sentence, one claim: "Rota done before the coffee machine warms up" (only if timed) |
| 9 | **Hype question** | "Ready to take your team to the next level?" | Use a real buyer question (pattern 6 above) or a statement |
| 10 | **Unsourced superlative** | "The world's most powerful rota app" | Use the measured fact, or drop the claim |
| 11 | **Fits everyone** | "Built for teams of all sizes" | Name the buyer: "For cafés with 3 to 15 staff" |
| 12 | **Where X meets Y** | "Where scheduling meets simplicity" | Say what it does: "Staff pick shifts, the rota fills itself" |

Two more tells to catch on sight: an em dash reveal ("Meet Shiftbook — your AI rota") counts as a
colon-reveal, and "Introducing X" as a headline says nothing about the reader.

## 5. Email subject lines and deck titles

**Email subject lines**

- Under 50 characters so it survives a phone inbox.
- Same gates as a hero headline except length. Sentence case.
- Say what is inside: "Your beta invite for Shiftbook" beats "Big news!"
- No fake reply or forward markers ("Re:", "Fwd:") on a first send.
- No urgency the facts do not support (see the honest-scarcity rule in `SKILL.md`).
- Preview text continues the subject; it does not repeat it.

**Deck titles**

- The title slide states the claim the deck proves: "Café rotas take 3 hours a week; we cut it
  to 10 minutes" (sourced) rather than "Shiftbook: Q1 Investor Update".
- Each slide title is a sentence that states that slide's point. A reader skimming titles only
  should get the argument.
- Same gates: no colon-reveal, no stock words, sentence case.
